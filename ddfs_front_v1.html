Design-day method (note 31 catalogue)

Choosing the method is a first-class DDFS step: different methods pick different days (the 30th busy hour may fall in July while the peak day falls in August). The method module computes each candidate from the full-year schedule and Sabre passenger weights and recommends; it is queued, and a single-day demo cannot compute it, so the table states requirements rather than approximating.

The methods in plain English (note 32, the client report standard text)

Why airports do not build for their single busiest moment. Every airport has one busiest hour of the year. Build for it and the terminal stands half empty the other 8,759 hours; build for the average and the airport is overcrowded for weeks every summer. Planners pick a level in between, and the methods below are different ways of picking it. They can give noticeably different answers: at Zagreb in 2015 the busiest hour fell in January, the 30th busiest hour in May, IATA's busy day in July and the busiest whole day in September.

The peak hour and the peak day. The single busiest hour or day of the year. Useful as a reference; the industry advises against designing to it, because you pay for space used a handful of times a year.

The 30th busiest hour (Standard Busy Rate). Sort all 8,760 hours busiest first and read off the 30th. About once every twelve days the airport is busier than this. A long-standing UK standard, easy to audit; needs full-year data. Sisters: the 20th hour (Amsterdam) and the 40th (Paris).

The 5% busy hour (Busy Hour Rate). Keep adding up the busiest hours until you have counted five percent of the year's traffic; the level reached is the design level, so no more than one passenger in twenty meets busier conditions. Heathrow's current practice; anchored to how many people experience the crowding.

The IATA Busy Day. Busiest month, then a typical week inside it, then that week's second busiest day; its busiest hour is the design hour. The method in IATA's own manual; gives a real day you can inspect flight by flight, and skips the wildest day. The picking steps must be written down; ours are.

The FAA busy month average day (PMAD). Average the busiest month's days into one typical day and take its busiest hour (a weekday-only variant exists). Smooth and stable; but an average day is not a real day.

The 90th-percentile day. Rank all days busiest first and take the one a tenth of the way down; about a month's worth of days a year will be busier. Simple to say, simple to defend.

One design day per season. Strongly seasonal airports get a summer day and a winter day (our Bologna practice). Doubles the outputs; only worth it where the seasons genuinely differ.

The rule-of-thumb ratio (FAA TPHP). A look-up from annual passengers; instant, and a sanity check, never the governing method where real schedule data exists.

Movements or passengers. Runways and stands care about aircraft movements; terminals and security care about passengers. The engine computes both; the Zagreb tables above are the worked example of why.

The Zagreb oracle: the sent deliverable regenerated in sample mode

The sent ZAG Secondary Forecast (18 June 2026) regenerated end to end from the pack inputs plus OAG schedules, as a test of the bridge's conventions against real deliverable numbers. 825 of the 995 rows carry regenerated values; 170 are flagged "GA input" rather than filled (general aviation stand demand is an input assumption in the sent workbook, not derivable from schedules). Where a value differs, the difference is a named convention under test, not an unexplained residual; the open conventions and their tested candidates are listed below the table.

Fidelity, stated precisely (reconciliation of 23 July 2026, ACI review item 1). Overall 30th-BHR growth matches the sent deliverable almost exactly (2-way total 2045/2025: sent x2.124, oracle x2.116), so the forecast-year growth application is not the residual's source. The residuals decompose into: (a) a base-year peak concentration of +13.4% on the 2-way total 30th hour, a property of the two-week sample annualisation, proven on ZAG 2015 real data (+6.6% share inflation at rank 30, growing with rank depth), which the full-year 2025 store resolves mechanically at load; the flat 0.80 loading against per-route load factors is the stated candidate for the remainder; and (b) a Non-Schengen composition difference: the sent Non-Schengen BHRs grow x1.55 against annual split paths both at x2.02 (Schengen x2.21), a peak-spreading behaviour the carrier-group growth mechanism only partly reproduces; whether her split basis is aircraft or final destination is a question for Jess alongside her three open conventions. The oracle is deliberately not tuned to the sample-week base (target-anchoring risk, note 29). Rung 1 of the ladder prints this decomposition on every run.

The bridge proposes, the analyst disposes. Adjustments apply to a named scenario branch; the Base day never moves. Demand-side levers change events and recompute everything live; supply-side levers change the frame the day is judged against (Checks and stand cover), not the schedule.
